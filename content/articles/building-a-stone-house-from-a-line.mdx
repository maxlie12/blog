I wanted to see one thing with my own eyes: could a person **drag** a single line across the grass
and get a stone house back? No menus, no grid, no picking parts from a list. Just one **stroke**.

Games such as Tiny Glade made this idea famous, and its developers have talked about how they build
theirs. This article is not about their code. I have not seen it, and my prototype almost certainly
works differently. This is about the small version I made with an AI coding assistant: one page of
JavaScript and three.js, drawing a little island in the browser. Every number below comes from that
file.

![A small floating island at sunset with two round stone houses, two towers, a low wall with stone arches, a pond and trees.](/images/tiny-valley-dusk.jpg)

*The opening scene of the prototype. It is built by the same steps a player uses, not modelled by hand.*

## 1. What "procedural" means here

**Procedural** art is made by rules. Instead of drawing each stone, I wrote the rule for where a
stone goes, and the computer follows it a thousand times.

The opening **diorama** has 1,414 stones, and nobody chose where any of them should go. A person
chose one line. The rules did the rest. That is the whole trick, and it is why the idea feels like
magic. A small movement of your hand gets a large, detailed answer back.

The rest of this article follows one line from your hand to a finished house, in seven steps.

## 2. Step one: from a shaky hand to a clean line

A real mouse path is messy. It has hundreds of points, it wobbles, and it stops and starts.

The page keeps a new point only when the pointer has moved at least 0.5 units from the last one. On
this island, one unit is roughly one metre. That removes most of the noise.

Then two passes of an old method called Chaikin's algorithm **smooth out** the corners. For every
pair of points, it keeps two new points, one quarter and three quarters of the way along. Each
pass doubles the number of points and cuts every corner a little. No matter how shaky the line is,
the wall looks calm afterwards.

The code asks one more question: did the line come back to where it began? If the ends are less
than 1.8 units apart and the line is longer than 7 units, the page treats it as a closed loop. Once
the line closes, it stops being a garden wall and becomes the **footprint** of a house.

That single test is the most important game design decision in the file. The player never presses
a "house" button. They just close a shape.

## 3. Step two: stacking the stones

Real walls are built in **courses**, so the code builds them the same way. It walks along the line
and **lays** one row at a time. Each row is 0.225 units higher than the last, and each stone gets a random
length between 0.3 and 0.64.

Random lengths do something useful: the joints in one row do not line up with the joints in the
next. Every row also starts at a slightly different offset, so that no gap runs straight up the
wall. Builders do this to make walls stronger. Here, it just makes the wall look believable.

Each stone also gets small random changes in size, angle and colour. Without them, the wall looks
like a printed pattern.

Random numbers create a problem, though. If stones were truly random, the whole wall would change
every time something nearby was rebuilt. So each wall gets its own **seed**, based on its ID. As
long as the seed stays the same, the stones stay the same. The wall looks random, but it can be
rebuilt exactly.

Behind the stones sits a simple core: thin boxes every 0.24 units. In a garden wall, the core is
dark **mortar**, and you see it in the gaps. In a house, it is cream plaster, and the stones stop
after the first metre or so. That one change is why the same code gives a stone wall and a white
cottage.

## 4. Step three: a path that opens the wall

This is the rule that feels most like magic. If you **drag** a path through a wall, the wall opens.

The code checks every segment of every path against every segment of the wall. When two segments
cross, it records the distance along the wall. That distance becomes a gap. If two crossings are
closer than 1.5 units, they count as one.

The gap itself comes from one small function. For a point on the wall, it answers a question: where
does the wall begin here? Far from a gap, the answer is the ground. Inside a gap, the answer follows
the top of a circle. So the rule **carves out** a doorway with a curved top, and any stone below
that curve is simply never placed.

An opening is not an **arch** yet, though. Nine lighter stones are then placed around the curve,
each one **tilted** to point at the centre of the circle. The middle one sits where a **keystone**
would be. In a garden wall, you can see through. In a house, a wooden door goes in the gap.

Notice what is missing. There is no "arch" object in the code that the player places. The arch
happens because two things touch. Tiny Glade has a similar behaviour, where a footpath drawn up to
a wall can grow an arched gateway ([Yahoo](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/gorgeous-cozy-castle-doodling-game-191627261.html)).
Results that appear from simple rules feel better than results chosen from a menu.

## 5. Step four: a roof from any shape

A closed loop can be any shape, so the roof cannot be a fixed model. The idea is to grow it from
the outline itself.

First, the code finds the **centroid** of the loop. Then it imagines nine rings. The first ring is
the outline, pushed outward a little to make the **eaves**. Each ring after that is smaller and
higher, and the last one is a single point above the centroid.

The height follows a curve, `H * t^1.5`. Here, `t` goes from 0 at the edge to 1 at the top. Because of
that power, the roof starts almost flat and gets steeper as it climbs. The result is the soft bell
shape of a storybook cottage, and it comes from a single number. If you change 1.5 to 1, you get
a plain cone.

The size of the house decides the height: the square root of its area, times 0.62, kept between 1.5
and 3.4 units. A larger **footprint** gets a taller roof with no extra input from the player.

Then the roof is covered in **shingles**. On each ring, the code works out how many fit, and every
second ring shifts by half a tile, the same way the stones do. Each **shingle** has to lie on the
slope, so the code takes two points, one on the ring below and one on the ring above, and uses them
to calculate its angle. The shingles are made 1.42 times longer than the ring spacing, so that each
one overlaps the row below.

## 6. Step five: drawing thousands of things cheaply

Each stone has 70 corners or so. If every stone were a separate object, the browser would have to
give the graphics card a separate instruction for each one.

**Instancing** solves that. The code sends the shape of one stone once, plus a long list: position,
rotation, size and colour for each copy. The graphics card then draws them all from a single call.
The prototype has eight of these lists, for stones, boxes, **shingles**, leaves, trunks, flowers,
windows and grass. Room is reserved for 42,000 stones, but the opening scene uses far fewer.

The animation uses the same list. Each stone stores the time it should appear, and those times are
**staggered** along the wall. A small piece of shader code grows each stone from nothing, with a
slight **overshoot**, so that it pops up like something alive rather than simply fading in. The wall
seems to build itself from one end to the other, and the roof follows from the **eaves** upward.

This is the only part of the file that is there just for pleasure, and it may be the most important
part. Watching your line turn into a wall is the reward.

## 7. Step six: undo for free

The easiest way to write undo would be to save a copy of the world after every action. The prototype
does something simpler.

It stores only what the player did, as a short list:

```js
{ type: "wall", closed: true, pts: [[-4.2, -2.2], ...] }
{ type: "path", pts: [[3.2, 11], ...] }
```

To show the world, the page **replays** the list from the start: clear everything, then build each
action again. **Replaying** sounds slow, but it was the simple choice, and because each wall has its
own **seed**, the result is exactly the same.

One decision then pays for four features:

- *Undo:* remove the last action and rebuild.
- *Erase:* remove any action near the pointer and rebuild.
- *Save:* write the list to the browser's storage.
- *Arches:* add a path, rebuild, and every wall finds its new gaps by itself.

The opening scene is the same kind of list, so it has no special code at all.

## 8. Step seven: light does most of the work

Grey stones on green grass look like a school project. Good light is what changed that.

The time slider blends four settings: morning, noon, sunset and night. Each one sets the sun's
position, colour and strength, plus the colour of the sky. At sunset, the low sun throws long
shadows, so every stone in a wall suddenly has shape. At night, the windows glow. There is no image
of a sky anywhere. It is a colour gradient behind a transparent canvas.

There is one small trick on the ground. A soft dark line is painted under every wall, where light
would struggle to reach. It costs almost nothing, and it stops buildings from looking as if they
are floating.

## 9. What I tested, and what still breaks

I played the prototype through an automated browser on my laptop, without a real graphics card:

- Starting scene: 1,414 stones and 2 houses
- Drawing a closed loop added a house and 102 stones: 1,516 and 3 houses
- Undo returned to 1,414 stones and 2 houses
- Dragging a path through a garden wall opened it: 1,466 stones, and a page reload kept the result
- No page errors, no sideways scrolling at phone width, and no button under 44 pixels

Those checks prove that the idea works. They do not prove that it is fun, and I have not played it on
a real phone. The simple rules also **break down** in places:

- *Concave houses.* The roof rises towards the **centroid**. On a star or an L shape, that point
  may fall outside the walls, and the roof folds over itself. This **edge case** needs a different
  method.
- *Separate roofs.* Two touching houses do not join. Each one gets its own roof.
- *Flat walls on hills.* All walls in a house stop at one height, so a house on a slope just has a
  taller wall on the low side.
- *No keyboard building.* You can move the camera with the keys, but drawing needs a mouse or a
  finger.

This test is a private page. A larger version of the idea, [Làng Rêu](/learning/village), now has its
own page on this site. The numbers in this article describe only the small test.

## 10. What I took from this

The code is about 470 dense lines, and the most interesting thing is what it leaves out. There is no stone model,
no roof model and no arch object. There are only a few rules that respond to each other.

**Procedural** work moves your effort. You spend less time making things and more time deciding what
should be true about them: stones should not line up, a closed line means a house, and a path
through a wall means a door. The player feels those decisions, not the maths.
