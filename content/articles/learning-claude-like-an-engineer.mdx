## A fast answer is not the same as a working solution

Imagine asking an AI assistant to fix a lesson page. It writes code, reports success and shows a
clean screenshot. Then you reload the page, and your answer disappears.

The screenshot was real. The solution was incomplete.

There is also a second, quieter problem. Even when the fix works, you may not have learned anything
from it. Next time a similar bug appears, you are back where you started and you depend on the tool
again.

This article is about both problems: getting work you can trust, and keeping your own skills growing
while you use AI. The scenarios are illustrations, not measured results from a real project.

## 1. What a 2026 study found

In January 2026, Judy Hanwen Shen and Alex Tamkin at Anthropic published a study on how AI help
affects the way people build coding skills. It was a **randomized controlled trial**: 52 software
engineers, mostly junior, were placed at random into two groups. All of them used Python at least
once a week, and none had used Trio, a Python library for asynchronous programming.

Both groups had 35 minutes to build two features with Trio. One group could ask an AI assistant in a
sidebar; the other wrote the code by hand. Then everyone took a quiz without AI on the concepts they
had just used. The quiz covered debugging, reading code, writing code and understanding the ideas.

The AI group scored 50% on average; the hand-coding group scored 67%. The difference was
statistically significant, and the largest gap was in debugging questions. The AI group finished
about two minutes faster, but that difference was not statistically significant.

In other words, in this task the help did not clearly save time, and it went with weaker
**comprehension** afterwards.

## 2. Read the caveats before the headline

It would be easy to turn this into a slogan: "AI makes you worse at coding". That is not what the
study shows, and the authors list its limits clearly. Each **caveat** matters:

- The **sample size** was small: 52 people.
- The quiz came straight after the task. It does not show whether the difference lasts, or whether it
  disappears as people gain experience.
- The assistant was a chat in a sidebar, not an agent that edits files by itself. The authors think
  agentic tools could have a larger effect, but they did not test that.
- It measured one library and one kind of task, and it did not compare AI help with help from a
  person.

Other 2026 data does not tell a single, simple story either. In June, Anthropic's Economic Index
surveyed about 9,700 Claude users. Of them, 68% said they were learning more with AI, and heavy
delegators said they were learning at the same rate as everyone else. But those answers are
**self-reported**, the people who answered were not a representative sample, and the report itself
says it cannot rule out a loss of skills.

So the two sources measure different things. One tested understanding in a controlled setting; the
other asked people how they feel. Just because a study is small does not mean we should ignore it,
and just because many people feel they are learning does not mean their skills are growing. Both are
useful, as long as we know what each one measured.

## 3. It is not the tool; it is how you ask

The most practical part of the January study is its closer look at behaviour. The researchers looked
at how each person in the AI group used the assistant, and found six patterns.

Three patterns went with low quiz scores, below 40% on average:

- *Full delegation:* the person let the AI write all the code. This was the fastest pattern, with
  few errors during the task.
- *Growing reliance:* the person started with a few questions, then handed all the coding to the AI.
- *Debugging by AI:* the person used the AI to fix and check errors, not to understand them.

Three patterns went with scores of 65% or higher:

- *Generate, then understand:* the person asked for code, then asked follow-up questions until they
  understood it.
- *Code with explanations:* the person asked for code and an explanation together.
- *Concept questions only:* the person asked only about ideas and fixed the errors themselves. This
  group was also fast.

Each pattern had only a few people, between two and seven, and the authors say the analysis does not
prove that the patterns caused the scores. Still, the shape is hard to ignore. It is not that people
who used AI learned less; it is that people who let the AI do the thinking learned less. Full
**delegation** produced code quickly, but it left less behind in the person's head.

The authors argue that some **productive struggle** is part of real learning. They suggest using AI
in ways that keep the **cognitive effort** in your own hands, and point to learning modes that tools
such as Claude Code and ChatGPT offer.

A March 2026 Anthropic report adds a hint from everyday use. Users who had joined at least six months
earlier were more likely to work with Claude in a back-and-forth way and less likely to simply give
orders, and their conversations were a few percentage points more likely to succeed. The authors warn
that early users may simply be more technical, that people who stopped using Claude are not counted,
and that "success" was judged by Claude itself. It is a pattern worth noticing, not proof.

## 4. A ladder of help

Here is one way to use these ideas. When you are learning something new, climb a ladder of help one
step at a time, and stop at the lowest step that gets you moving.

![A ladder of five steps, from the least help to the most: a hint, a concept, a review of your attempt, a worked solution, and full delegation only for work you already understand.](/images/claude-practice/help-ladder.png)

1. *A hint.* Ask the AI to **hold back** the solution and give one hint about where to look.
2. *A concept.* Ask it to explain the idea you are missing, with a small example that is not your
   code.
3. *A review.* Write your own attempt, then ask for a review of your **diff**: what is wrong, and why.
4. *A worked solution.* Only then ask for a full solution, and ask it to **walk through** each part.
5. *Delegation.* For work you already understand well, letting the AI write the code is fine. The goal
   of that task is output, not learning.

This ladder is a form of **scaffolding**: temporary support that you remove as your skill grows. It is
my suggestion, built on the study's patterns. The study did not test it.

Try to explain the bug in your own words before you ask for help. If you can explain the problem
clearly, you probably understand half of the fix already.

## 5. Give the task a clear boundary

When the goal is a working change rather than learning, the most useful habit is a short task brief:
describe the symptom, the expected behaviour, the permitted **scope** and how to check the result.
Include what must not change.

For example: "Save this draft after a refresh. Keep older attempts. Do not change the database or
deploy anything. Test an empty draft, an edited draft and a storage failure."

Those three tests include **edge cases**: unusual situations where code often fails. A plausible fix
usually handles the normal case. The edge cases are where you find out whether it really works.

An **assumption** might still be wrong. Perhaps the app has several learner profiles. Perhaps storage
is blocked in a private window. The assistant needs permission to investigate the relevant code, not
an instruction to read every file without a reason.

[My guide to working with Claude](/blog/hoc-claude-lam-viec-voi-ai) covers briefs, context, project
instructions and security in more detail.

## 6. Evidence, not "Done"

Developers already know that AI output needs checking. In Stack Overflow's 2026 Developer Survey,
published in October with more than 30,000 respondents in 169 countries, 66% said they use coding
assistants or coding agents. When asked how much they trust the output, 48% chose the answer that
they trust it when they can easily check it. Only 6.6% said they trust it for many tasks including
important work decisions.

Claude Code's best-practices page names the same risk. It calls it the trust-then-verify gap: the AI
produces code that looks right but does not handle edge cases. Its advice is direct: always give a
way to **verify** the work, through tests, scripts or screenshots, and do not ship what you cannot
verify. It also suggests asking Claude to show evidence, such as test output or the exact command it
ran, rather than simply saying it succeeded.

Even AI checking AI needs care. In a March 2026 post about long-running agents, an Anthropic engineer
reported that Claude, used as a tester without special tuning, found real problems and then talked
itself into approving the work anyway.

Before I approve a change, I read the diff. Then I ask three questions:

- What **evidence** shows that the problem is solved?
- What did this check not cover?
- Could I explain this change to another developer?

Just because the build passes does not mean the microphone works. A build, a storage test and a
real-device check answer different questions. Record the missing checks instead of hiding them behind
"Done". On this site, every change brief ends with a section for exactly that: what was not checked,
and what risk remains.

## 7. Keep ownership of the learning

After a task, **step back** and explain why the change belongs in that file. Then try a slightly
different case without asking the AI to write the answer.

Debugging deserves extra attention, because that is where the January study found the largest gap.
When a bug appears, give yourself a fixed time, perhaps fifteen minutes, to **figure out** the cause
before you ask for help. Read the error, make a guess, test it. Use AI to **look up** a concept or
check your reasoning, not to replace it.

The newest tools make this harder, not easier. Anthropic's June 2026 report found that people gave
Claude Code more independence than the chat app on the same kinds of work. For blog posts, for
example, the typical chat conversation had 13 rounds of back-and-forth, while the typical Claude Code
session had a single prompt. One prompt can be efficient. But if you are trying to learn, a single
prompt also means a single chance to think.

So be careful not to **rely on** AI for the part you are trying to learn. What matters is whether you
could do a similar task with less help next month.

Keeping **ownership** means knowing what you accept responsibility for. It does not mean writing every
line alone. It means that your **judgement**, not the tool's confidence, decides what ships.

## 8. A weekly routine

Here is a small routine for one week. It is a suggestion, not a tested programme.

- *Monday:* pick one real bug. Write the symptom and your guess before you open the AI.
- *Tuesday:* use the ladder of help. Note which step finally got you moving.
- *Wednesday:* write one test for an edge case before you ask for a patch.
- *Thursday:* read a diff that the AI wrote, and explain it aloud in English in five sentences.
- *Friday:* redo a similar small task with less help, and compare.

Twenty minutes a day is enough. Volume is not the aim; noticing where your understanding is still thin
is.

## Try it yourself

Speak for 45 seconds: describe one small development problem and how you would check a fix. Then
write 80–100 words explaining which step on the ladder of help you would use first, and why. Do not
use AI to rewrite your first version.

For feedback, ask an AI assistant for one reasoning issue and one language issue only. No score or
progress claim is implied.
