Most of us learned English the same way: read the textbook, highlight the important parts, and
reread everything the night before the exam. It felt like hard work. It turns out that much of it
was the wrong kind of work. The methods that work best are often **counterintuitive**: they feel
slower and harder while you use them.

This article collects what cognitive psychology and human biology say about learning — any skill,
not only a language — and turns it into a short daily routine that I use on this site.

## 1. A simple learning loop

Language researchers often describe two sides of learning. Stephen Krashen argued that we acquire
a language from **comprehensible** input: messages we can understand, a little above our current
level. Merrill Swain added that output — speaking and writing — makes us notice the gaps in what
we know ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensible_output)). Researchers still argue
about how much each side matters, so I use both.

Put together, practice becomes a loop: *input* (read or listen to something you mostly
understand), **retrieve** (recall it without looking), *output* (use it in your own sentence),
*feedback* (find one important mistake), and review again later. The same loop works for code,
music or sport.

## 2. Psychology: what actually works

In 2013, John Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten popular study techniques. Only two received a
"high utility" rating: practice testing and distributed practice. Rereading, highlighting and
summarizing were rated low
([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)).

Why do we still reread? Because of **familiarity**. When a text looks known, we feel that we know
it. But recognizing a word on the page is not the same as producing it in a conversation.

*Retrieval practice.* Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke showed that taking a test does not
only measure memory — it improves it. On delayed tests, students who practised recalling
remembered more than students who simply studied again
([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/repeated-retrieval-during-learning-is-the-key-to-long-term-retent/)).
Each time you **retrieve** a word, it becomes easier to find next time. So, instead of rereading
your notes, close them and write what you remember. **Retrieval** is the exercise; checking the
notes afterwards is the feedback.

*Spacing.* We forget fast. Ebbinghaus measured his famous forgetting curve in the 1880s, and a
2015 replication found a very similar curve
([Murre & Dros, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0120644)).
In a study of more than 1,350 people, the best gap between two study sessions depended on how long
you need to remember: roughly 20–40% of the time for a test one week later, but only 5–10% for a
test one year later ([Cepeda et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)). In
practice, **space out** your reviews: the next day, a few days later, a week later, a month later.
**Spacing** feels slow, but it **pays off**.

*Interleaving.* When students mixed different types of maths problems in one session instead of
practising one type at a time, they did much better on a test a week later, even though practice
felt harder ([Rohrer & Taylor, 2007](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1767/)). For
English, **interleaving** means mixing tenses, skills and topics instead of doing twenty identical
exercises.

*A popular myth.* Many people believe they are "visual" or "auditory" learners. A major review
found virtually no evidence that matching teaching to a learning style improves results
([Pashler et al., 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)). The **myth** is
harmless only until it stops you from listening or speaking. Choose the format that fits the
content, not your "style".

All of these methods are not only harder but also slower at first, which means that your feeling
of progress is a poor guide. The words you **struggle with** today are often the ones that
**stick** later.

## 3. Motivation: make practice automatic

Motivation goes up and down, so do not rely on it. A plan with a trigger works better. In a
meta-analysis of 94 tests, "if–then" plans — called implementation intentions — had a
medium-to-large effect on reaching goals
([Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/05_Sheeran_Webb_Gollwitzer_Goal_Intention_Implementation.pdf)).
Write it like this: *When I sit down at my desk after lunch, I will review five words.* The
situation is the **cue**; the action is small and clear.

How long does a habit take? In a University College London study of 96 people, it took about 66 days
on average for a daily behaviour to reach **automaticity**, with a range from 18 to 254 days. Missing
a single day did not stop the habit from forming
([Lally et al., via PsyBlog](https://www.spring.org.uk/2023/01/form-a-habit.php)). A missed day is
a **setback**, not a failure. That is also why Done and the streak on this site are a habit
record, not a level score.

Practice time is not everything, though. A large meta-analysis found that deliberate practice
explained 26% of performance differences in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports and only 4% in
education ([Macnamara et al., via Princeton](https://www.princeton.edu/news/2014/07/03/becoming-expert-takes-more-practice)).
Hours matter, but *how* you practise matters too.

## 4. Biology: your brain needs sleep and movement

Learning does not end when you close the book. In two experiments with high school students
learning vocabulary, memory was better when sleep came within a few hours after learning, and sleep
**deprivation** still hurt memory after a night of recovery sleep
([Gais, Lucas & Born, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16741280)). During sleep, the brain
**consolidates** new memories. A simple way to use this **consolidation**: learn new words in the
evening, sleep, then retrieve them the next morning before you look.

Movement helps, too — with a caveat. A review of 29 studies found that a single session of
exercise **boosts** BDNF, a protein linked to the brain's ability to change, with a moderate
effect ([Szuhany, Bugatti & Otto, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25455510/)). But BDNF
was measured in the blood, and a direct link to better language learning has not been proven. A
ten-minute walk before studying is cheap and safe, not a magic pill.

## 5. AI tutors in 2026: scaffolding or a crutch?

AI chatbots are now part of how many people learn English. A 2025 meta-analysis of 41 studies
reported a moderate-to-large positive effect of generative AI chatbots on second language learning
([Li et al., 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.70060)). But a 2025 randomized
trial with 120 students, in a non-language course, found that the group who studied with ChatGPT
scored lower on a later retention test than the group who studied traditionally (57.5% vs 68.5%)
([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125010186)).

The difference is how you use it. When AI does the retrieval for you, it becomes a **crutch**.
When it gives feedback *after* you try, it works as **scaffolding**. Even if an AI can write a
perfect sentence, you still need to produce one yourself. Do not **outsource** the thinking **to**
the tool — try first, then ask it to correct one important mistake.

## 6. My 20-minute routine

1. Recall (3 min): write yesterday's words and one sentence from memory, then check.
2. Input (7 min): one short text or audio at my level — one lesson on Learning.
3. Output (5 min): three to five sentences, or 30 seconds of speaking with the recorder.
4. Feedback (3 min): ask ChatGPT to correct the single most important mistake.
5. Plan (2 min): mark Done and decide when I will see these words again — tomorrow, in three
   days, next week.

None of this needs a new app or more willpower. It needs a better kind of effort: recall instead of
reread, small and often instead of long and rare, and sleep instead of one more hour of study.
