Some games make your heart race. Others do the opposite: you build a small house, you plant a tree,
nothing attacks you, and half an hour later your shoulders have dropped. Many people now pick those
games on purpose to **unwind** after work.

The internet has a simple story about this: **cozy** games reduce stress. I wanted to know how much
of that story is true, because this site now has a small building game, [Làng Rêu](/learning/village),
next to the learning. It is one thing to feel calm; it is another to prove why. So this article has
two parts: what researchers and designers think is happening, and how strong the evidence is.

The short answer: the ideas are useful, but the proof is weaker than the headlines suggest.

## 1. What "cozy" means

The most cited definition comes from game designers, not from scientists. In a 2017 working group at
Project Horseshoe, a designer think tank, the group wrote that coziness "refers to how strongly a
game evokes the fantasy of safety, abundance, and softness"
([Lostgarden](https://lostgarden.com/2018/01/24/cozy-games/)). So coziness refers to a feeling the
game **evokes**, not to a type of game.

Each of the three parts means something concrete:

- *Safety:* "a cozy game has an absence of danger and risk." Nothing important can be lost.
- **Abundance**: you are not struggling for food, shelter or money, so you have room to think about
  other things. **Abundance** is why a cozy game rarely asks you to count your resources.
- *Softness:* colours, sounds and shapes "tell players they are in a low stress environment."

Tiny Glade fits well. The team says it has "no combat or management pressure," and its design
pillars include "no wrong answers"
([80 Level](https://80.lv/articles/exclusive-tiny-glade-developers-discuss-bevy-proceduralism-publishers-cozy-games/),
[GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/how-tiny-glade-built-its-way-to-600k)). You
cannot fail. You can only make something you like more or less.

Notice that "safe" here is a design choice. A cozy game *removes* the things that most games use to
create tension: timers, enemies, scores and losing.

## 2. Idea one: three psychological needs

One useful explanation comes from self-determination theory, a well-known theory of motivation from
Richard Ryan and Edward Deci. It proposes that people do best when three basic needs are met:

- **Autonomy**: you feel that your actions are your own choice.
- **Competence**: you feel able to do things well.
- **Relatedness**: you feel connected to other people.

In 2010, Andrew Przybylski, Scott Rigby and Richard Ryan used this theory to argue that the appeal
of games comes from their potential to satisfy these needs
([Review of General Psychology](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/a0019440)).

It is easy to see how a building game could fit. You decide what to build, and nobody tells you
otherwise, so there is **autonomy**. A small action produces a beautiful result, so there is a feeling
of **competence**. Think about your day: an inbox full of other people's requests, and work that never
quite finishes. A game can give back the exact feelings that the day took.

I should be clear about the status of this. It is a theory that fits well, and it does not prove that
any one game helped any one person. Also, a lone building game does little for **relatedness**.

## 3. Idea two: how people recover

A second idea comes from work psychology. Sabine Sonnentag and Charlotte Fritz studied how people
**recover from** work, and they described four experiences
([Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 2007](https://www.sowi.uni-mannheim.de/en/sonnentag/research/measures-and-tools)):

- **Detachment**: your mind stops thinking about work, not just your body leaving the office.
- *Relaxation:* low activation with pleasant feelings.
- **Mastery**: a challenge outside work that builds a skill.
- *Control:* you decide what to do with your free time, and how.

Now look at a **cozy** game again. It takes enough attention to pull you away from your problems,
which helps **detachment**. It asks nothing urgent, which helps relaxation. It gives you choices, which
helps control.

Scrolling a phone feels like rest, but it often gives you none of these. That is my own reading,
not a finding from those papers. Still, the four experiences give you a better question than "was
that relaxing?" You can ask: did my mind really leave work?

## 4. What the evidence says

This is where I have to slow down, because the research on **cozy** games specifically is thin. Much
of it is small, and much of it is student work or interviews. A student thesis at Utrecht University
says plainly that there is still little research on this topic. So let me start with larger studies
of games in general.

*A 2021 Oxford study.* Niklas Johannes, Matti Vuorre and Andrew Przybylski worked with two game
companies. This matters, because they got **telemetry**, real records of play, and not only memories.
For *Animal Crossing: New Horizons* they had 2,756 players, and for *Plants vs. Zombies: Battle for
Neighborville* 471. They found "a small positive relation" between play time and feeling good. They
also found that people's own estimates of play time were too high on average, which shows why a
**self-report** can mislead. But this was a **correlation** at one point in time. In their words,
"we cannot claim that play time causally affects well-being." Happy people may simply play more.

*A larger follow-up in 2022.* This team followed 38,935 players of seven games for six weeks. They
"found little to no evidence for a causal connection between game play and well-being." That is an
important result, and it does not **back up** the popular story. Hours played did not seem to change how
people felt, in either direction.

But the same study found something more interesting: "motivations play a role." Players who played
because they enjoyed it, **intrinsic** motivation, felt better afterwards. Players who felt pressure
to play, **extrinsic** motivation, felt worse. This suggests that why you play may matter more than
how long. The authors call their causal claims tentative, and these were not **cozy** games
specifically.

*A small interview study.* In 2025, George Farmer and colleagues published 13 interviews about
games during the COVID lockdowns. People described games as something that **made up for** what
lockdown took away: choice, connection and progress. The paper's title says it well: "a good
distraction from the mayhem of reality." The same people also described **guilt** about "wasting"
time, and some said that their play could turn harmful. Thirteen young, mostly male volunteers
cannot speak for everyone, and the authors say so.

Here is what I can honestly say:

- Many people report that **cozy** games help them relax. I believe them.
- Large studies do not show that more game time improves **well-being**.
- Why you play seems to matter more than how much.
- I found no strong trial showing that **cozy** games reduce stress better than something else.

You may see a claim online that **cozy** games work as well as meditation. I tried to find the
original study and could not verify it, so I left it out. One study could not **rule out** other causes
anyway, and a good feeling after a game is not the same as better long-term health.

## 5. The limits of a nice feeling

There is one more honest point. **Escapism** is not always healthy. A game may help you rest, but
it cannot fix what is making you tired. If a pleasant island is the only place you feel okay, the
island is not the thing to look at.

A useful test comes from the 2022 result. Rather than count hours, ask yourself one question: do
I want to play right now, or do I feel that I have to? If a calm game has started to feel like a
duty, it has stopped being **cozy**, whatever it looks like.

## 6. What this changed for my learning site

This research changed one real decision.

The Project Horseshoe group wrote something that matters here: "almost any form of **extrinsic** reward
generates a pressing transactional short-term need." In plain English, a reward can quietly turn a
pleasant activity into a job. Points, streaks and daily bonuses all create a small voice saying
"don't lose it."

The more a reward pushes you, the less the task feels like yours. That is exactly the risk of joining
a game to a learning app. If reading an article earned coins, then I would be teaching people to
read for coins. The 2022 motivation result points the same way, though it was about games and not
learning.

So these are the rules I am using for now:

- *The game stays separate.* Nobody needs it to learn, and it never changes Done or a level.
- *No coins for reading an article.* In Làng Rêu, learning a word opens the piece that word names,
  so the reward is the word itself. If that ever **undercuts** the learning, it goes. The farm here
  does give a small gift for saved practice, so that is the part to watch.
- *No punishment.* A rest day creates no debt, in the lessons or in a game.
- *Honest labels.* Done on this site is a habit mark. It is not a level, and a decoration will
  never mean that your English improved.

A reward can also lose its pull once the novelty **wears off**, which is one more reason not to build
a learning habit on it.

## 7. What I took from this

I could not confirm the popular claim that **cozy** games reduce stress. What I found was more useful. A **cozy** game is a place with no danger, enough of everything, and choices that are yours.
Those conditions are worth copying, even outside games.

They are good conditions for learning too. You make more mistakes when you feel safe enough to
make them, and you come back when coming back is your own choice. I cannot prove that with these
studies, so treat it as my view. It is the view I am going to build on.
