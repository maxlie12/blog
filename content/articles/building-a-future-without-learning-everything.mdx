## Too many useful things

Picture a developer with a full-time job, some freelance work, an English goal, a Chinese goal and
two personal projects. Every item makes sense on its own. Together, they ask for more hours than one
person has.

The usual reaction is to try harder: wake up earlier, buy another course, install another app. But
the problem is often not motivation. It is **capacity**: the time, attention and energy that are
really available in a normal week, after work, sleep and family.

This article argues for a different approach. You do not need to learn everything to build a
future. You need to choose what gets real attention now, and to be honest about what waits.

It is written in the first person because it is a viewpoint, not a rule. The examples are
illustrations, not a record of anyone's career.

## 1. What the job data says, and what it cannot say

Much of the pressure to learn everything comes from a fear of being left behind. So it is worth
looking at real data before reacting to headlines.

One of the best-known surveys of employers is the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report.
Its 2025 edition asked more than 1,000 employers, who together employ over 14 million workers, across 22
industry clusters and 55 economies. They expect new jobs equal to 14% of today's employment to be
created by 2030, about 170 million, and jobs equal to 8% to disappear, about 92 million. The net
change is a gain of about 78 million jobs. The report calls the total movement, new jobs plus lost
jobs, a **churn** of 22% of today's jobs.

Skills move too. On average, employers expect 39% of workers' existing skills to change or become
outdated between 2025 and 2030. That is lower than the 44% they expected in 2023, but it is still a
large share.

The report also imagines the world's workforce as 100 people. By 2030, 59 of them would need
training. Employers expect to give 29 of them **upskilling** in their current roles and to move 19
into other roles inside the same organisation. But 11 would be unlikely to receive the training they
need.

Which skills are rising fastest? AI and big data come first, followed by networks and cybersecurity,
and technological literacy. The list also includes creative thinking; **resilience**, flexibility
and agility; and curiosity and lifelong learning. Analytical thinking remains the most important core
skill: seven in ten companies call it essential.

Now the limits. This is a survey of what employers expect, not a forecast for one person. It is
global, so it does not describe the Vietnamese job market or any single company. It cannot tell you
that a particular job title is safe or doomed.

Still, one number deserves attention: 11 people out of 100 may not get the training they need from
an employer. If your company may not train you, you need a learning plan that you can run yourself.
And a plan you run yourself has to be small enough to survive a busy month.

## 2. What 2026 research adds about AI skills

Two pieces of research from this year make the picture more concrete.

The first comes from Fabian Stephany, a researcher at the Oxford Internet Institute, writing for the
World Economic Forum in February 2026. In a study of more than 10 million UK job postings, roles that
asked for AI skills advertised salaries about 23% higher than similar roles without them. That
**premium** was larger than the premium linked to a Master's degree, about 13%, or a Bachelor's
degree, about 8%. In a separate experiment, 1,700 hiring professionals in the US and the UK compared
CVs that were identical except for AI skills. Candidates who listed AI skills were 8% to 15% more
likely to be invited to an interview, and the effect was stronger when a recognised certificate
backed the skills up.

Those results need care. Advertised salaries are not actual pay. An interview invitation is not a
job offer. And the article presents the author's views, not an official WEF position. But the
direction is clear: being able to work well with AI is becoming a valuable addition to a skill set.

The second piece comes from Anthropic itself. In January 2026, Tristan Hume, an engineer there,
described what happened to the company's take-home test for performance engineers. Newer Claude
models kept beating it. One model matched even the strongest human candidates within the time limit.
The team redesigned the test several times. According to the post, the job now leans on hard
debugging, systems design, performance analysis, checking that code is correct, and making
AI-written code simpler.

That is one company and one specialist role, so it is not a rule for every developer. But it points
in an interesting direction. When AI can produce a lot of code, what stays valuable is the judgement
around the code: understanding the problem, finding the real cause of a bug and deciding what is
good enough to ship.

## 3. Why "learn everything" backfires

It is easy to make a list of useful skills, but harder to give each one enough attention to grow.

Part of the reason is the cost of moving between tasks. In 2001, the psychologists Joshua
Rubinstein, Jeffrey Evans and David Meyer ran experiments in which young adults switched between
tasks, such as solving maths problems and sorting shapes. People lost time on every switch, and they
lost more when the tasks were complex or unfamiliar. The American Psychological Association calls
these **switching costs**. Meyer has estimated that such small mental blocks can add up to as much as
40% of someone's productive time. That number is an estimate quoted by the APA, not a measurement of
your job, but it shows why the costs matter.

The tasks in those experiments were small and artificial. A career is not a lab task. Still, the
idea transfers: moving between a bug at work, a freelance client, an English lesson and a side
project is not free. Each switch asks you to reload the rules of a different world.

So when you **spread yourself too thin**, you do not get four quarter-results. You may get four
projects that never reach the point where they are useful. Not because you are lazy, but because each
one keeps restarting.

The more projects you carry, the less attention each one receives. That is not a law of nature, but
it is a useful warning sign.

## 4. A strong core before a longer list

For a frontend developer, a useful **priority** may be understanding an entire user journey: the
interface, the API it calls, the failure states, and the way to investigate a problem when something
breaks. That is a core skill. It creates value in almost any team, and a short tutorial cannot
replace it.

Learning another framework can help when a task needs it. It is not automatically the next step.

Instead of opening another project, I can finish one useful task. I can learn the missing technical
idea while solving it, and then explain the result in my own words without asking AI to speak for me.
The explanation is part of the skill. If I cannot explain a fix, I probably do not own it yet.

Both sources above point the same way. Analytical thinking tops the employers' list, and the
Anthropic test now rewards debugging and judgement. Neither of those is a separate course. They grow
when you work on real problems and look closely at what happened.

## 5. Three layers, not three full-time jobs

One way to organise attention is to think in layers.

The first layer is the core: a skill that creates value and supports a **livelihood**. For many
developers, that is the work they are paid for.

The second layer is **leverage**: abilities that make the core more valuable. Clear communication,
English for meetings and documents, and responsible use of AI tools all belong here. They do not
replace the core. They help other people understand and use it. The salary research in section 2
suggests that AI skills now sit firmly in this layer.

The third layer is an **option**: one small personal experiment that might open a different path
later. It could be a tiny game, a blog or a short course in a new field. The point is to keep it
small enough that it does not compete with the first layer.

Under all three is the life that supports you: rest, health and the people you care about. They are
not tools for higher productivity; they matter on their own.

The diagram is a thinking tool, not a measured formula. The layers do not need equal time. In a
demanding month, the second and third layers may shrink to almost nothing, and that is acceptable.

## 6. Learn to make a trade-off

A **trade-off** means that choosing one thing leaves less room for another. Every week contains
**trade-offs**, whether you name them or not. Naming them is better.

If freelance work has a real deadline, a personal feature can be **put on hold**. If a new job needs
attention, a certificate can wait. Pausing is not the same as abandoning an idea.

A simple tool is a list with three parts: continue, pause and stop.

- *Continue:* things that support the core, or that have a real **commitment** behind them, like a
  client deadline.
- *Pause:* useful things without a deadline. Write down where you stopped, so that you can return.
- *Stop:* things you keep only because you would feel guilty dropping them.

The pause list needs a home. Put those ideas in a **backlog**, a list you are allowed to ignore for
now. A learning plan should help you return after a busy period, not punish you with an endless list
of things you **fell behind** on.

Be careful with new things, too. Before you **take on** a new course or project, ask what it will
replace. If the answer is "nothing", the time will come from sleep, rest or the core.

## 7. Turn one goal into an if-then plan

Good intentions are easy to start and easy to forget. One well-studied technique is to link a goal
to a specific situation.

The psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran call these plans implementation intentions.
In a 2006 meta-analysis of 94 independent studies with more than 8,000 participants, people who made
if-then plans reached their goals more often than people who only set the goal. The average effect
was medium to large.

The format is simple: if a situation happens, then I will do a specific action.

- If it is Tuesday after dinner, then I will write 100 words about one bug I fixed this week.
- If I open my laptop for freelance work, then I will first write today's single goal on paper.
- If a new course looks exciting, then I will add it to the backlog and decide at the monthly review.

These examples are mine, not from the research. The studies cover many kinds of goals, so they do
not prove that this exact plan will work for a developer. But it is cheap to try, and two weeks are
enough to see whether it helps.

## 8. Prepare for change without pretending to predict it

Nobody can tell you which tools will matter in 2030. What you can do is learn to **adapt**: build
useful skills, explain your work, keep professional relationships and review your direction when
circumstances change.

Adapting is easier when there is evidence of what you can do. A small **portfolio** helps: a few
short write-ups, in English, of problems you solved, what you tried and what you learned. It shows
your thinking, not just a list of technologies. The hiring experiment above also found that
recognised proof made AI skills count for more, and a write-up is one kind of proof you can make
yourself.

A portfolio also helps you **keep up with** change without chasing every trend. When a new tool
appears, ask one question: does it make my core work better, or is it only new? If it is only new, it
can wait.

AI tools are a good example. They are part of everyday development now, and learning to use them well
is real leverage. I wrote separately about [how to work with Claude](/blog/hoc-claude-lam-viec-voi-ai)
and [how to keep learning while you use it](/blog/learning-claude-like-an-engineer). The aim is to use
the tool to grow the core, not to replace it.

The goal is not to know everything, but to keep learning.

## 9. Choose a pace that can last

A **sustainable** week does not need a new product, a finished course and a perfect study streak. It
can contain good work, one lesson you understood, some exercise and time with people you care about.

Speed feels productive, but the pace has to survive bad weeks too. If a plan only works when nothing
goes wrong, it is not a plan for real life. The World Health Organization describes **burnout** as a
syndrome that comes from long-term work stress that has not been managed well. It treats it as a work
phenomenon, not as a medical condition. This article cannot diagnose anything. If you feel exhausted
most of the time, talk to someone you trust or to a health professional.

One small habit helps. When you notice you are overloaded, **prioritize** by cutting, not by working
longer. Remove one thing for two weeks and see what happens.

A monthly review keeps the plan honest. Answer three questions in a few lines:

1. What became easier this month?
2. What still needs help?
3. What should I stop carrying?

The answers will change from month to month. That is information, not failure.

## Try it yourself

Write 100–120 words in three parts: one thing to continue, one thing to put on hold and one skill to
improve. Give a reason for each, and add one if-then plan. Use at least three words from this article.

If speaking is easier, talk for one minute first and then write. Keep your first draft. If you want
feedback, ask an AI assistant for one unclear reason and one language problem, not for a full rewrite.
