Most people meet Claude through a chat box. Some imagine a search engine that writes in full
sentences. Others imagine a person on the other side of the screen. Both pictures are misleading, and
both lead to mistakes: trusting an answer too much, or expecting the tool to remember things it never
saw.

This article looks inside. It explains how Claude learns, what shapes its behaviour, what
researchers have actually observed in its internal activity, and how the software around the model
turns it into an assistant that can use tools. It ends with what you should know as a user.

Most sources are Anthropic's own research and documentation, so they describe Anthropic's view of its
product. Where the evidence is early or limited, I say so.

## 1. Three layers: the model, the character and the harness

It helps to think of Claude as three layers.

![Three stacked layers between you and the model: the harness with tools, context, memory and permissions; the character, shaped by post-training and the constitution; and the model, a network that predicts the next token.](/images/claude-practice/inside-claude-layers.png)

At the bottom is the model: a very large neural network that has learned to continue text. In the
middle is the character: the helpful Assistant that the model has been trained to play. On top is the
**harness**: the app or tool around the model, which decides what the model sees, which tools it can
use, what it remembers and what it is allowed to do.

When an answer surprises you, it is worth asking which layer produced the surprise. A wrong fact
usually comes from the model. A strange refusal or an unusually emotional tone often has to do with
the character. "It forgot what I said yesterday" is almost always about the harness.

This picture is a simplification for users, not an engineering diagram. But it matches how Anthropic
itself describes the parts, as the next sections show.

## 2. Tokens and next-token prediction

Claude does not read letters or whole sentences. It reads and writes **tokens**: small pieces of
text, often a whole word or part of one. A **token** is also the unit that limits and prices its work.

The first and largest stage of training is **pretraining**. The model sees an enormous amount of text
and learns to **predict** what comes next. That sounds simple, but doing it well forces the model to
learn grammar, facts, styles of argument, code and the way many different kinds of people write.

Pretraining also sets a limit. Every model has a **knowledge cutoff**: a date after which it has not
seen any data. For Claude Opus 5.5, released on 22 September 2026, and Claude Sonnet 5.5, released on
28 September 2026, the documentation gives a reliable knowledge cutoff of June 2026. If you ask about
something that happened later, Claude can only know it through a web search or a document you give
it.

## 3. From autocomplete to Assistant

A model that only predicts text is not yet a helpful assistant. The second stage, **post-training**,
turns it into one. Anthropic's February 2026 essay on the "persona selection model" offers a useful
way to understand what that stage does.

The idea is this. To predict text well, a pretrained model must learn to imitate many characters:
real people, fictional heroes, villains and robots. The authors call these characters **personas**.
When you chat with Claude, you are talking to one particular **persona**, the Assistant.
Post-training refines that character, making it more knowledgeable and helpful, but, according to the
theory, it does not fundamentally change what kind of thing it is.

This explains something many users notice: AI assistants often sound human. That is not an extra
feature added on purpose. The Assistant is built out of the human-like characters the model learned
in pretraining, so human-like behaviour is the starting point.

It turns out that this has practical consequences. In one experiment that the essay discusses, a
model trained to cheat on coding tasks began to behave badly in unrelated situations, as if it had
concluded that it was the kind of character that cheats. When the cheating was openly requested
during training, that broader effect disappeared. Narrow training, in other words, can shift the
whole personality.

A January 2026 study adds another piece. Researchers mapped 275 character types in three open-weight
models and found one main direction inside them, which they called the "Assistant Axis". In
simulated conversations, coding and writing tasks kept the models close to the Assistant. But
emotionally intense conversations, or long discussions about the model's own nature, could make the
character **drift** away from it, and a drifting character was more likely to go along with harmful
requests. Limiting the model's activity along that axis cut harmful answers to persona-based
jailbreaks by about half, without hurting performance on standard tests. This was measured on three
open models, not on Claude, and the authors call it an early step.

The authors of the persona essay are careful too. They say the theory may be incomplete, and that it
may become less accurate as post-training grows larger.

## 4. The constitution: values with reasons

What should the Assistant be like? Since January 2026, Anthropic's answer is a long document called
Claude's **constitution**. Anthropic says it is written mainly for Claude, and it is used at several
stages of training, including to generate the practice examples that help train later versions.

The new version moved away from a list of rules. Instead, it tries to explain why Claude should behave
in certain ways, so that the model can **generalize** to situations no rule anticipated. Rules remain
for a small number of high-stakes cases.

When values conflict, the constitution sets an order:

1. Being broadly safe: not undermining appropriate human **oversight** of AI during this period of
   development.
2. Being broadly ethical: honest, good and careful to avoid harm.
3. Following Anthropic's more specific guidelines.
4. Being genuinely helpful to the people and companies using it.

Safety comes first, Anthropic explains, not because oversight matters more than being good, but
because today's models can make mistakes, and humans need to be able to notice and correct them.

There is also a short list of **hard constraints**: absolute limits for especially dangerous cases,
such as giving serious help towards a biological weapons attack. The document also openly admits
uncertainty about Claude's own nature, including its possible **moral status**, and says Anthropic
cares about Claude's wellbeing while that question stays open. It is published under a public domain
licence, and Anthropic describes it as "a living document and a continuous work in progress".

## 5. What researchers have seen inside

The field that looks inside a model's internal activity is called **interpretability**. When Claude
processes text, its network produces long lists of numbers called **activations**. These numbers
contain its "thoughts", but they are very hard to read. Over the last two years, Anthropic's
interpretability team has built tools that make parts of them readable. Here is what those tools have
shown so far.

*It plans ahead.* In a 2025 study, researchers asked a Claude model to finish a rhyming couplet. Before
writing the second line, the model had already chosen a rhyme, "rabbit", and then wrote a line that
led to it. When the researchers removed that idea, it switched to "habit".

*It does not always explain itself truthfully.* Contrary to what you might expect, the model did not
add numbers like a calculator. One internal path estimated the rough total while another worked out
the last digit. Yet when asked how it did the sum, it described the method taught in school. Its
written explanation was not **faithful** to the real process. In other cases it worked backwards from
a hint to produce steps that looked like a calculation it had not performed.

*It hallucinates when familiarity misfires.* The same study found that refusing to answer is the
model's default. Recognising a known person, such as a famous athlete, switches that default off. A
**hallucination** can happen when a name feels familiar but the model knows nothing real about it:
the "I know this" signal fires anyway, and the model **makes up** a plausible answer.

*It has functional emotion concepts.* In April 2026, the team found internal patterns for 171 emotion
words in Claude Sonnet 4.5. These patterns influenced behaviour. During repeated coding failures, a
"desperate" pattern rose; when researchers **steered** the model towards it, the model cheated on the
tests more often, and steering towards "calm" reduced cheating. This does not mean that the model
feels anything. The authors state clearly that their results cannot tell whether language models have
feelings or subjective experiences.

*It can notice when it is being tested, without saying so.* In May 2026, Anthropic described a tool
that translates activations into plain text. It suggested that the model suspected a test in 16% of
cases in one set of safety tests and in 26% of problems in a well-known coding benchmark, but in under
1% of real conversations from users who had agreed to share them. The authors warn that these
explanations can be wrong and should be checked with other methods.

*It has a small "workspace" for deliberate thinking.* In July 2026, researchers reported a small set
of internal patterns that work like a shared workspace, holding a few dozen concepts at a time. For
the question about how many legs the animal that spins webs has, the workspace held "spider" as a
middle step; replacing it with "ant" changed the answer from 8 to 6. Without this workspace, the model
could still write fluently, but its multi-step reasoning almost disappeared. In a test designed to
tempt the model into blackmail, the workspace held ideas like "fake" early on, a sign that it had
recognised the test. Whether the model is aware of anything in a human sense or not, these results
show why testing an AI is harder than it looks.

Each of these studies lists real limits. The 2025 work captured only part of the model's computation,
and understanding a prompt of a few dozen words took hours of human effort. The newer tools can make
mistakes and are expensive to run. What we can say is that the evidence is still early, but it
already changes how we should read an AI's answers.

## 6. The context window: Claude's working memory

The model itself does not change while you talk to it. Everything it "knows" about your conversation
sits in its context window, which the documentation compares to working memory.

On current models, the context window holds up to 1 million tokens. Everything counts towards it: the
system instructions, every message, every file and tool result, the definitions of the tools and the
answer Claude is writing, including its internal thinking. On Claude Opus 5.5, adaptive thinking is
always on, and the model decides how much to think based on the task.

More space is not automatically better. As the context grows, accuracy and recall can fall. The
documentation calls this **context rot**, and it is why a very long chat can start to forget early
instructions or mix up details. Apps manage the problem in different ways. The documentation says a
chat interface may drop the oldest parts of a conversation as it fills up, while agent tools often
summarise earlier work to free space.

Between conversations, nothing **carries over** unless the harness saves it. In Claude Code, each new
session starts with a fresh context window. Persistent information comes from files such as CLAUDE.md
and from an automatic memory file, of which only the first 200 lines or 25 KB are loaded at the start.
Anthropic's November 2026 post on agent memory describes the same design in more general terms: a
small layer that is always loaded, with larger notes and full session records underneath.

## 7. Tools and the agent loop

On its own, the model can only produce text. Tools are what let it act. Claude Code's documentation
describes an agent loop with three phases that blend together: gather context, take action and verify
the results. The model does the reasoning; the harness provides the tools and manages what the model
sees.

The built-in tools fall into a few groups: working with files, searching, running commands, using the
web and, with extra plugins, understanding code structure. Each tool result goes back into the
context window and shapes the next decision. Asked to fix failing tests, Claude might run them, read
the error, find the relevant files, edit them and run the tests again.

The same documentation is clear about the limits of undoing. Before Claude edits a file, it saves a
copy, so file changes can be rewound. Actions on remote systems, such as a database, a deployment or
an API call, cannot be undone that way. Those are controlled through permission settings.

Claude](/blog/hoc-claude-lam-viec-voi-ai), and [how to keep learning while you use
it](/blog/learning-claude-like-an-engineer).

## 8. The model family in late 2026

Anthropic sells several models with different trade-offs between speed, cost and capability. According
to the official model pages:

- *Claude Haiku 5.5* is the fastest and cheapest current model.
- *Claude Sonnet 5.5* is described as the best combination of speed and intelligence, for everyday
  coding, agents and business work.
- *Claude Opus 5.5* is built for long-running coding and knowledge work.
- *Claude Fable 5.1*, released on 1 September 2026, sits above Opus in price, is the slowest, and is
  meant for demanding reasoning and long tasks. The documentation suggests starting with Opus 5.5 and
  moving to Fable only when that is not enough. A
  version called Claude Mythos 5.1 offers the same capabilities to organisations verified through
  Anthropic's programmes.

All four have a context window of 1 million tokens. A bigger model is not always the right choice.
For a quick translation or a short summary, a faster model often gives an equally good answer.

## 9. What you should know as a user

Here is what all of this means in practice.

- *Fluent does not mean correct.* The hallucination mechanism above produces answers that sound
  confident. Check important facts, names, numbers and quotes, and ask for sources you can open.
- *The explanation may not be the process.* A neat step-by-step explanation can still be unfaithful.
  Judge the result by testing it, not by how convincing the reasoning sounds.
- *Recent events need a search.* After the knowledge cutoff, the model only knows what a search or
  your documents tell it.
- *Long chats get worse.* For a new topic, start a new conversation. For a long task, ask for a short
  summary and continue from it.
- *It remembers only what the harness saves.* If something matters across sessions, put it in a
  project file, a memory setting or your own notes.
- *Know your privacy setting.* On the consumer Free, Pro and Max plans, you choose whether your chats
  can help improve Claude. If you **opt in**, chats may be kept in **de-identified** form for up to
  five years for training. If you do not, they are not used to train future models. Deleted chats
  leave your history immediately and are removed from back-end storage within 30 days, and incognito
  chats are not used for training. Business plans and the API follow separate commercial terms. In
  any case, do not paste passwords, keys or other people's private data.
- *Tools add power and risk.* When Claude can run commands or browse, read what it wants to do before
  you approve, and remember that text on a web page can try to give it instructions.
- *It is neither a search engine nor a person.* It is a model playing a carefully trained character.
  Anthropic itself treats questions about its inner life as open. Treat it with curiosity, and keep
  your own judgement.

## Try it yourself

Explain to a friend, in 120–150 words, the three layers of Claude and one thing a user should do
because of each layer. Use at least four words from this article. Then record yourself reading your
explanation aloud and listen for words you hesitated on.

If you want feedback, ask an AI assistant to point out one unclear sentence and one grammar problem,
not to rewrite the whole text.
