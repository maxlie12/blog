Tiny Glade is a game about drawing little castles. In less than a month after its launch in
September 2024, it sold 616,000 copies on Steam
([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/how-tiny-glade-built-its-way-to-600k)). It was
made by two people.

It did not begin as a castle game. It began as one wall.

That fact is useful to anyone who wants to plan a whole project before starting it. This article is
about what that wall really teaches, and also about what it does not.

## 1. The story

In 2024, the game's developers, Anastasia Opara and Tomasz Stachowiak, spoke to the site 80 Level.
Opara said it "started as a hobby weekend project to test rendering," while she still had a
full-time job at Embark Studios
([80 Level](https://80.lv/articles/exclusive-tiny-glade-developers-discuss-bevy-proceduralism-publishers-cozy-games/)).
80 Level had noticed the project back then, when it was only a wall generator made in a game engine
called Bevy.

So a **side project** tested one narrow thing. It did not need a story, a market or a business
plan. What started as a weekend wall generator became a full game.

The next step matters most. They did not simply decide it was a game. They
checked. They posted it on TikTok to see whether people cared, and it "picked up a lot of interest."
Even they were puzzled. Stachowiak said they were "a bit surprised as to why people wanted to just
play a game where you draw walls."

In other words, the audience answered a question that the developers could not answer alone.

## 2. Four things that happened next

The story continues with some good lessons about how a small project grows.

*The tools got replaced.* Eventually the game **outgrew** Bevy. Stachowiak said "our needs
outscaled what Bevy could provide at the time." They wanted prettier graphics and better generation
on the GPU, and they moved to Vulkan, a lower-level graphics technology. That is a healthy order. They picked an easy tool, proved the idea, and took on the harder work only when
the project demanded it.

*Tools were rebuilt many times.* Some features went through "five or six iterations" in the 80
Level interview. In a later newsletter, the developers said "some tools went through 6 or 7 fully
fleshed out prototypes." The numbers differ a little, but the point is the same. A good result was
usually not the first version. Every **iteration** meant being willing to **throw away** something
that already worked.

*Testing changed the plan.* The developers described beginning with "a simple version that we can
test on ourselves." They also ran five external **playtests** in two years. Bridges were "not
planned at all in the beginning," but players asked for them, and they grew into a wider system for
raising buildings. In **playtesting**, people used stairs in an unexpected way, which **led to** a
new tool. Neither of those could have appeared in a plan.

*They set rules before building.* The team chose design **pillars** early: "a lot from little
effort," "no wrong answers" and "it's alive." A **pillar** gives you a way to say no. If a new idea
needs a lot of effort from the player, it fails the first test, however clever it is. That is the
best defence I know against **feature creep**.

## 3. What this story cannot teach

Now the warning, because it would be easy to get this wrong.

It is tempting to read this story as a recipe: make a small thing, post a video, sell 600,000
copies. That is **survivorship bias**. We are reading about this wall generator because it
succeeded. Nobody interviews the developers of the thousand weekend projects that went nowhere.

The numbers show this too. The game launched with 1,375,441 **wishlists**. Very few projects will
ever have that, and it was not luck alone. Both developers came with a strong **track record** in
rendering and procedural generation at large studios. A weekend wall by two experts is not the same
as a weekend wall by a beginner.

They also did not stop at something small. The newsletter describes a two-year development period,
and the polish of the final game very likely helped it sell.

So "start small" is not a promise of success. That does not mean the advice is useless. It means
the benefit is different from what the story suggests. Starting small does not make you more likely
to get famous. It makes you more likely to find out quickly whether you are wrong, and more likely
to finish something.

## 4. My own wall

I have a smaller example from this site, and it is not a success story.

I built a learning path, an ordered route of lessons, in the style of a language app. It worked. It
passed its tests, and the screenshots looked good. I put it on its own page, behind a small link.

Then I looked for it as a learner and could not find it. The feature **turned out** to be useless,
not because the code was bad, but because I had tested the wrong thing. I checked that it worked.
I had not checked that someone could get to it. The fix was small: I moved it onto the main Learning
page. If I had opened the site as a normal learner sooner, I would have saved a whole round of work.

There is a good example on this site too. When I added speech recognition, I
[picked one small job](/blog/i-put-a-speech-model-inside-my-browser): turn a recording into a text
draft. I **scaled back** from "AI feature" to one button, and that small test **paid off**, because
it showed the real costs quickly.

## 5. A method you can use

Here is one way to apply this. It works for code, and section 6 shows that it works for language
practice too.

1. *Write one question.* A **prototype** is an experiment, so it needs a question. "Can a dragged
   line become a believable wall?" is a question. "Build my game" is not. If you have only a
   **hunch**, turn it into something that could be proved wrong.
2. *Set a hard limit.* Give it a weekend, or one page of code. A **constraint** forces you to
   choose. When the limit is generous, unfinished work **piles up** and every idea feels important.
3. *Build only the part that is scary.* Skip the login, the settings and the logo. Build the
   thing you are not sure can work.
4. *Show a real person.* Watch them, and do not explain. The moment you say "no, click there," you
   have found a problem.
5. *Write down what you did not build.* New ideas will come. Put them in a **backlog**, so that
   you can stop thinking about them. Before adding a feature, ask whether anyone would notice if it
   were gone.
6. *Decide: stop, change or continue.* Stopping is a good result. You paid a weekend for an answer
   that could have cost you a year.

The smaller the test, the sooner you learn. A small **tweak** after one real conversation is often
worth more than a week of private planning.

## 6. The same rule for learning a language

This is where the wall connects to Learning on this site.

Language learners make the same mistake as developers. We plan the perfect system: every app, a
colour-coded notebook, one hour a day for a year. It looks great on paper, and it often lasts a week.
That is a **scope** problem.

The small version looks like a wall. Can you do five minutes today? Can you write one sentence, check
it, and fix one mistake? That is why the daily lessons here are small, and why a rest day creates no
debt. [My article on learning science](/blog/how-to-learn-english-with-learning-science) explains
why spacing and recall matter. Scope explains why you will still be doing them next month.

The method fits too:

- *One question:* "Can I explain a technical decision in five sentences?"
- *A hard limit:* ten minutes.
- *A real person:* say it to someone, or record it and listen back.
- *A decision:* keep the sentence pattern, change it, or drop it.

Your first version will be poor, like every **prototype**. The job of that version is to exist, so
that you have something to improve. You will never **ship** a perfect plan.

## 7. What I took from this

I do not think the lesson of Tiny Glade is "small things become big." Most do not. The lesson is
narrower: find the smallest thing that can answer a real question, and then let the answer,
rather than your plan, choose the next step.

One wall was enough to learn that people liked watching walls. Everything else came after that.

As a test of my own advice, I made
[a small stone house prototype](/blog/building-a-stone-house-from-a-line) with one question: can a
dragged line become a house? It has since grown into [Làng Rêu](/learning/village), a small building
game on this site. Whether people enjoy it is the next question.
